# Writing a 9:16 cartoon short

These are craft rules for 40–60 s narrated mini-stories, in plain words. They describe a shape that holds attention in
a vertical feed. Write your own story, lines and characters inside it. If the user shows a video they like, it
teaches the shape only, never its topic, lines, characters, story beats or frames.

## The one-sentence formula
**A concrete little story that seems to make no sense, told fast, with the "why" held back until the last 25 %, where
one true mechanism snaps everything into place.**

## The shape (55–60 s, about 160–185 words, ≈ 2.9–3.1 words/s)
| Beat | Time | Words | Job | Devices that work |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1 Hook** | 0–3 s | ≤ 20 | One sentence that sounds wrong, impossible or high-stakes on its own | a paradox ("⟨X⟩ is banned, yet it's everywhere"); a backwards outcome (the worst option turns out to be the lucky one); the middle of a strange act + one line of dialogue; a huge prediction ("when we invent ⟨X⟩, everyone…"); a detective promise ("there's a way to tell ⟨…⟩") |
| **2 Drop-in** | 3–10 s | 20–25 | Put the viewer inside one scene: one place, one person, 2–3 nameable objects | "Imagine …" / "So you …" (second person), or one protagonist named by role (the baker, the inventor, the cashier) |
| **3 Ladder** | 10–35 s | 70–80 | 3–5 escalating beats, each a new picture | "then … then … then all at once" (rule of three + acceleration); a **doubt beat** (someone laughs it off or explains it away); short in-character lines in a modern, casual voice |
| **4 Turn** | 35–48 s | 40–45 | The hidden mechanism that reframes every earlier beat | opens with **"You see,"**; one physical, legal or psychological cause, explained with one everyday picture (a coin slot, a queue, a thermostat) |
| **5 Kicker** | 48–60 s | ≤ 15 | One line that lands the consequence, the irony, or a question to the viewer | an ironic payoff (the rule is technically never broken); a dark twist; a direct question; or stop mid-thought so the last words flow back into the hook (a **loop**) |

**With a generated voice**, plan for a slower read. Our two examples came out at about 2.6–2.7 words/s (131 words →
49.7 s; 171 words → 63 s), and 60 s scripts first came back at 73–77 s. Write to the low end (about 150–160 words for
60 s) and expect to cut a sentence after the first take. Never speed the voice up.

## The 11 rules
1. **Every sentence is a shot.** Use concrete nouns and verbs we can build and animate: a wheel of cheese, a little
   steel hammer, a vault door swinging open. If a sentence has no picture, give it one or cut it.
2. **Withhold the why.** Story first, explanation last. Explain nothing in the first 60 % of the runtime. Why: the open
   question is what keeps people watching.
3. **One mechanism per video.** One cause, stated once, plainly, with one everyday analogy. Why: two causes split the
   payoff, and neither lands.
4. **Escalate by repetition with a twist.** The same action three times, faster or bigger each time.
5. **Doubt before vindication.** Someone dismisses it ("Your friends think it's a joke."); the Turn pays it off. Why:
   the viewer gets to be right with the story.
6. **A casual modern voice, even in history.** Contractions, understatement, plain words for fancy things, a light
   joke. It keeps the story friendly.
7. **Second person for systems, a protagonist for stories.** "You roll your best wheel into the bank…" for a hidden
   system; "the inventor", "the cashier" for a mini-tale.
8. **"You see," is the hinge.** Use it once, at the Turn. A second time at most.
9. **Numbers: few and vivid.** One or two, said the way a person says them ("hundreds of thousands of wheels").
   Each one is sourced in FACTS.md, and any figure on screen is exactly what the voice says.
10. **Short sentences, spoken rhythm.** About 12–15 words on average, contractions, no list longer than three.
11. **End hard.** No outro, no "subscribe", no recap. The kicker is the end; a loop back into the hook is a bonus.

## One paragraph, always
Write the script, and show it to the user, as **one continuous paragraph**: no line breaks, no one-sentence lines, no
stat cards. Join sentences with and / so / because / but where the thought runs on. Keep at most one or two real
fragments, usually the doubt line or the kicker. Shot breaks belong in the shot table, not in the script. Why: the
voice reads it as one breath of story, and the user judges it as a story, not a list.

## Worked example: the cheese short (F1, 171 words, 63 s)
The script of `src/examples/cheese-bank-vault/`, as one paragraph:

> In Italy, one bank will lend you money if you bring it a giant wheel of cheese. Imagine you make Parmesan. Every
> wheel has to sit on a shelf for at least a year before you can sell it, but your bills are due now. So you roll
> your best wheel into a marble bank. A man in a suit taps it with a little steel hammer and listens for air bubbles
> inside. He nods, and a huge vault door swings open on shelves of cheese all the way to the ceiling, hundreds of
> thousands of wheels. Every day, people walk the aisles, checking them for cracks. Your friends think it's a joke. A
> bank full of cheese? You see, this cheese gets more valuable the longer it ages. The bank keeps your wheels in its
> vault and ages them for you. And if you can't pay the loan back, it simply sells the cheese and gets its money back.
> Finally, a loan backed by something that gets better with age.

| Beat | Voice time | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | 0–4.2 s | a paradox: a bank that wants cheese |
| Drop-in | 5.2–17 s | "Imagine you make Parmesan": one maker, one wheel, one bank |
| Ladder | 17.9–38 s | the hammer test → the vault opens → the aisles checked every day (each a new picture) |
| Doubt | 39–42.3 s | "Your friends think it's a joke. A bank full of cheese?" (the two allowed fragments) |
| Turn | 43.3–57 s | "You see," at 69 %: ageing makes the collateral worth more |
| Kicker | 57.7–62 s | the irony in one line |

Every real claim in it (the year of ageing, the hammer test, the shelves, the daily checks, the sale on default) has
a source in its FACTS.md. "Your friends think it's a joke" is story, and marked so.

## Topic buckets (pick ideas that fit one)
| Bucket | What it is | Mechanism type |
|---|---|---|
| Hidden systems / loopholes | how a real rule is quietly bent | legal, economic |
| History with a mechanism | an old invention or custom told as a mini-story | physical, social |
| Everyday science paradox | why people or things behave against intuition | psychological, statistical |
| Sci-fi "what if" | a future invention with a nasty hidden cost | philosophical |
| Parables / logic puzzles | a clever person outwits power | logical |

## Titles
3–7 words: a specific noun plus a mystery. Pick title case or sentence case once per channel and keep it. Never all
caps, no emoji.
- "The ⟨Object⟩ That ⟨Did Something Odd⟩"
- "Why ⟨Group⟩ Keep ⟨Doing X⟩"
- "The ⟨Rule / Place⟩ Nobody ⟨Notices⟩"
- "What If We Invented ⟨X⟩?" (F3)
- "Levels of ⟨X⟩" (F4)

Our example: "The Bank Vault Full of Cheese". idea-lab writes the full packaging (titles, description, tags).

## Facts
- Every real-world claim gets a `verify:` note in the draft you show: what to check and where (an official body, a
  medical reference, a paper, a reputable news report).
- The facts table lives in `projects/<slug>/FACTS.md`: claim · as spoken · source · checked date. Story lines (the
  doubt beat, invented reactions) are marked "story", not sourced.
- Numbers only when a source gives them, said with "about". Never invent one to make a beat land; use a comparison
  picture instead.
- A claim you can't verify is attributed ("some people say…") or cut, and listed in the delivery.
- `projects/<slug>/script.txt` holds the spoken words only; the `verify:` notes stay in FACTS.md and the draft.

## Script checklist (run before any voice is made)
- [ ] The hook is ≤ 20 words and sounds wrong, impossible or high-stakes on its own
- [ ] The viewer is inside a concrete scene by second 10
- [ ] 3–5 ladder beats, each a new picture; at least one doubt beat
- [ ] The "You see," Turn starts between 60 % and 80 % of the runtime
- [ ] One mechanism, one analogy; every real claim has a `verify:` note and a source in FACTS.md
- [ ] The kicker is ≤ 15 words; the ending loops back into the hook if possible
- [ ] 150–185 words for 55–60 s (≈ 2.9–3.1 words/s), the low end with a generated voice; read through once for rhythm
- [ ] One continuous paragraph; at most one or two real fragments
- [ ] Our cast only (Mika is the default "you"); no real people; nobody else's characters, lines or jokes
- [ ] Every sentence maps to a shot in the shot list (FORMATS.md)
