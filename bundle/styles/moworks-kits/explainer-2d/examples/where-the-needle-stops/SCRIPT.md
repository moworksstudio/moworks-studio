## Narration

A needle is about to go into your arm. In a second, it will push ten drops of medicine through
your skin. You can't just swallow it, because your stomach would digest many medicines, like
insulin, the same way it digests lunch. So we take a shortcut, and where the needle stops decides
how fast it works. In the fat under your skin, it seeps into your blood over hours. In muscle,
packed with tiny blood vessels, it takes minutes, and in a vein, just seconds. Then your heart pumps
it around your body in about a minute, all from one small sting.

103 words, 7 sentences, written as one continuous read (an earlier, choppier draft was rejected for reading as a
list). Median sentence 15 words, about grade 6. `story/script.txt` holds it word for word.

Length: about 35 s of speech at roughly 3 words per second, plus a 0.9 s lead-in, a 0.6 s beat after the last word
and about 4 s of title: 40 s. A slower or faster reader changes this; `tools/time-voice.mjs` prints the length to set.

## Fact sheet

Every number on screen or in the narration, and the fact behind it.

- A typical shot in the arm (most vaccines) is 0.5 mL. A standard medical drop is about 0.05 mL (20 drops per mL),
  so 0.5 mL is about ten drops.
- Insulin is a protein. Stomach acid and digestive enzymes break it down like food protein, which is why it is
  injected rather than swallowed.
- Absorption speed by route: into a vein (intravenous) the drug is in the blood at once and acts within seconds;
  muscle (intramuscular) has a rich blood supply, so absorption starts within minutes; the fat under the skin
  (subcutaneous) has fewer blood vessels, so absorption is slower and spreads over hours (for example, rapid-acting
  insulin peaks at 1–2 h and lasts 3–5 h).
- At rest, the heart pumps the blood around the whole body in about a minute.
